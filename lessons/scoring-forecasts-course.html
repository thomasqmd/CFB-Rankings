Scoring the power model's forecasts

Seven short lessons, from the posterior draws your model produces to the one number you put on the dashboard. Every example uses your own posteriors and backtest.

About 15–20 minutes per lesson. Data: 19,177 out-of-sample games, 2014–2026; the 2025 postseason posterior (4,000 draws); ablation refits, 2022–2025. Rebuild the data with Rscript assets/prep-course-data.R.

How to use this. Each lesson opens with a Predict first prompt. Commit to an answer before reading on, because a guess you've made, even a wrong one, makes the explanation stick. Then read, play with the widget, and take the short quiz from memory. Lesson 7 ends with a mixed review quiz. Take it a few days later, not straight away: recall after a gap is what builds long-term memory.

Lesson 1 · Summarise

Credible sets: four honest ways to draw an interval

Every rating, rank and probability on your dashboard carries an 80% interval. There are several valid ways to draw one, and they disagree exactly where it matters: for ranks and probabilities.

Predict firstNotre Dame finished 2025 most likely ranked 2nd, but with a long tail toward 20th. Will the shortest set of ranks holding 80% of the posterior be shorter or longer than the dashboard's interval (10th to 90th percentile), and which end will move?

1.1What a credible interval is

Stan gives you 4,000 draws of every unknown. A credible interval at level L is any interval holding probability L under the posterior.10 The statement is direct: given the data and the model, Indiana's rating is between a and b with 80% probability.9 "Any interval" is the catch, because many intervals hold 80%. The methods below differ only in which 80% they pick.

Credible vs. confidence. A 95% confidence interval is a statement about the procedure: across repeated datasets, 95% of intervals built this way would cover the true value. A credible interval is a probability statement about the parameter itself, given this data and this prior.9,10

Your pipeline puts intervals on two different kinds of thing, and it helps to keep them apart:

  • A parameter, such as Indiana's rating. This is a posterior credible interval: uncertainty about the team. Indiana's 2025 final rating has a posterior sd of 4.2 points.
  • An outcome, such as next week's margin. This is a posterior predictive interval: uncertainty about the teams plus game-to-game noise. Your margin forecasts have an sd of about 16 points.

Only the second kind can be checked against results, which is what Lessons 4 and 5 do. Everything in this lesson applies to both.

1.3See them disagree

Below are three real 2025 postseason posteriors and one made-up bimodal case. Shaded histogram bars mark the highest-density set. The four rows underneath draw each method's interval on the same axis.

What to notice:

  • Indiana's rating is symmetric, so all four methods agree (about 30 to 41 points). With symmetric posteriors the choice doesn't matter.
  • Notre Dame's rank: the equal-tailed interval is 1 to 9, but it actually holds 92% of draws. Rank 1 alone has 13%, so there's no bottom 10% to trim off. The highest-density set, 1 to 6, holds 81%. The normal approximation starts at rank −0.2.
  • Indiana's win probability against Notre Dame is bounded at 1 and skewed left. The HDI (0.51 to 0.91) slides toward the top compared with the equal-tailed interval (0.44 to 0.87). Raise the level to 95% and the normal approximation runs past 1.
  • The bimodal case: the equal-tailed interval and the HDI both cover the valley between the two modes, even though values there are unlikely. Only the highest-density set splits in two.
  • Drop to 200 draws. The faint ticks show how much the endpoints wander between random sets of 200, and the wandering is wider for 95% than for 50%. The tails have the fewest draws, so their quantiles carry the most Monte Carlo error. Check it with posterior::mcse_quantile() before reading the edges of a 95% interval too literally.12

1.4Your top 12, both ways

2025 postseason posterior, FBS ranks per draw. Each blue bar is the 10th–90th percentile range that the dashboard reports. Each set of orange blocks is the smallest set of ranks holding 80%. For teams near the top, the blue bar runs further down the rankings than the most plausible ranks justify.

1.5Change of scale: the equal-tailed interval survives, the HDI doesn't

Ratings turn into win probabilities through a monotone function: a bigger rating gap always means a higher win probability. Quantiles go along for the ride, so the 10th percentile of the rating gap maps to the 10th percentile of the win probability. HDIs don't, because squeezing or stretching the axis changes which window is shortest.9

Indiana minus Notre Dame, 2025 postseason posterior. Top: the rating difference per draw. Bottom: the implied neutral-field win probability per draw, using the model's scale-t margin law with its game-noise parameters fixed at their posterior means, which keeps the map monotone.

Takeaways
  • A credible interval is any interval with posterior probability L. The methods differ in which one they choose.
  • Equal-tailed intervals survive changes of scale and are what your pipeline reports. Keep them, and say so.
  • For ranks and probabilities near a boundary, the equal-tailed interval overcovers and points away from the most plausible values. Show the highest-density set when "most plausible ranks" is the real question.
  • Tail endpoints need many draws. Check their Monte Carlo error.

Lesson 2 · Score

The Brier score is one slice of the CRPS

Your model predicts a whole distribution for every margin. The score that grades all of it is built from Brier scores, and it turns into plain absolute error when the spread is thrown away.

GameModel saidResultBrierCRPS
Michigan at Ohio State, 2024OSU by 14.0, 82% to winMichigan 13–100.67510.4
Georgia vs. Florida State, 2023 Orange BowlUGA by 7.6, 69% to winGeorgia 63–30.09544.3
 Lower is better. Backtest means over 19,177 games: Brier 0.166, CRPS 9.20.
Predict firstThe two scores disagree completely. Which was the model's worse forecast? Commit to an answer, and we'll come back to it.

2.1The Brier score: squared error on a yes/no question

For an event with forecast probability p and outcome o (1 if it happened, 0 if not):1

BS = (p − o)²

Three anchors: 0 means you said 100% and it happened; 0.25 is saying 50% every time; 1 means you said 100% and it didn't happen. For The Game, (0.82 − 0)² = 0.675, worse than 97% of backtest games.

Why squared? Squared error is proper: your best expected score comes from reporting your true belief.2 Absolute error |p − o| is not. If you believe 70%, your expected absolute error is 0.7 + p(1 − 1.4), which keeps falling as p rises to 1, so it pays to exaggerate.

2.2Your model answers a yes/no question at every threshold

The predictive CDF F(t) = P(margin ≤ t) gives one probability per threshold. The win probability is just p = 1 − F(0). Every threshold also has an outcome, 1{y ≤ t}, which is a step at the final margin y. So every threshold has a Brier score, and the continuous ranked probability score adds them up along the margin axis:3,6

CRPS(F, y) = ∫ ( F(t) − 1{y ≤ t} )² dta Brier score per threshold, times a width in points, so the total is in points

At t = 0 the squared gap is exactly (p − o)². Your Brier score is the CRPS's slice at zero. The same holds after averaging: the mean CRPS is the area under the mean Brier score plotted against the threshold.4,7 Here is that plot for your whole backtest:

All 19,177 games. The area of 9.14 falls a little short of the mean CRPS of 9.20 only because the plot stops at ±80 points and uses 1-point steps. Gneiting, Balabdaoui & Raftery call this a Brier score plot.7

Now for single games. Hover over the lower panel: every vertical slice is one yes/no question with its own Brier score, and the purple slice is the only one the Brier score looks at.

2.3Throw the spread away and CRPS becomes absolute error

A point forecast m is a step at m, and the result is a step at y. The two steps differ by exactly 1 between m and y, so CRPS = |m − y|.6 MAE is the CRPS of a forecast with no spread. That puts your two numbers on the same scale: an MAE of 12.9 for the centre alone, and a CRPS of 9.2 for the full distribution.

Spread isn't free. When the centre is dead on, a Normal forecast with sd σ still scores about 0.23σ, or 3.5 points at σ = 15. The CRPS charges for width when you're right and refunds it when you're wrong.

From draws: CRPS = E|X − y| − ½E|X − X′|.2,8 In R that's scoringRules::crps_sample(y, dat = draws), or mean(abs(x - y)) - 0.5 * mean(abs(x - sample(x))).

Back to the two games. The Game was badly wrong on the one question the Brier score asks, but Michigan by 3 landed inside the model's own 80% interval, so its CRPS of 10.4 is close to average. The Orange Bowl picked the right winner, but every threshold from +8 to +60 was wrong: its CRPS ranks 33rd-worst of 19,177. If the question is who wins, use the Brier score. If it's by how much, use the CRPS, which already contains the Brier score as one of its slices.

Takeaways
  • Brier = (p − o)², a squared error on one yes/no question.
  • CRPS = the Brier score integrated over every threshold, in points. The Brier score is its slice at t = 0.
  • MAE = the CRPS with no spread. Your 12.9 → 9.2 is what the distribution earns.

Lesson 3 · Score

The log score: how much probability did you put on what happened?

Your paper chose the margin family with elpd, which is a log score, and backtests with CRPS. The two can rank models differently, and you need to know why.

Predict firstTwo forecasts have the same centre and the same standard deviation. One is Normal; the other is a Student-t with heavy tails. A result lands four standard deviations out. Which score will tell them apart: the CRPS, the log score, or both?

3.1Definition and units

LogS(F, y) = −log f(y)minus the log of the forecast density at the result · lower is better · units: nats

The log score goes back to Good (1952) and is strictly proper.8 It is also local: it looks only at the density at the result, and ignores how close the rest of the distribution came.6 The CRPS is the opposite. It looks at the whole CDF and measures distance.

Differences read as ratios. A log score 1 nat lower means the forecast put e ≈ 2.7 times more density on what happened. A mean difference of Δ across games is a geometric-mean density ratio of eΔ.

Three names, two directions. The log score −log f(y) is lower-is-better. elpd = Σ log f(y) is higher-is-better: it's the same quantity summed over games with the sign flipped, and loo estimates it out of sample.13 Your log loss of 0.498 is the log score of the binary win/loss outcome, not of the margin.

3.2Your two games

GameCRPS× backtest meanLog scoreDensity vs. a typical game
The Game, 202410.41.1×4.22same (mean 4.22)
Orange Bowl, 202344.34.8×11.0e6.8 ≈ 880× smaller
 Log scores come from a kernel density over the 199 stored quantiles, which is the method scoringRules::logs_sample() uses on draws. Kernel estimates are fragile far out in the tails,8 so treat the Orange Bowl's 11.0 as rough.

The CRPS rates the Orange Bowl about 5 times worse than average. The log score rates it about 880 times less likely than a typical result. The log score punishes surprises far harder.

3.3Tails: where the two scores part ways

Drag the result out past ±50. The Normal's log score grows like z²/2, which is quadratic, while the t's grows only logarithmically. Meanwhile both CRPS curves rise in near-parallel straight lines. The scoringRules authors make the same point with a gamma forecast: the log score climbs steeply away from the mode, while the CRPS rises more gently and more symmetrically around the median.8 The log score judges the shape of the tails. The CRPS mostly judges centre and width.

Random sample of backtest games. Game by game, the two scores agree almost perfectly on ordering. They differ in how much more a surprise costs.

3.4Why this matters for your margin family

Two pieces of your own evidence point different ways:

  • margin_comparison (in-sample LOO at each season's final posterior) prefers the scale-t by Δelpd ≈ 229 (se 30) (plans/todo.md).
  • The one-week-ahead ablation backtest for 2022–2025 has the plain Normal and Student-t margins slightly ahead of the published scale-t: ΔCRPS ≈ −0.05 points per game (Lesson 6).

These measure different things, on different data, with different scores. The scale-t's scale grows with the expected margin, which changes how the tails of lopsided games are shaped. A tail-sensitive log score can reward that while the CRPS barely notices. Neither result is wrong. When you report a model choice, say which score made it.

Takeaways
  • LogS = −log f(y), in nats. It is local and tail-sensitive. elpd is the same quantity summed, with the sign flipped.
  • CRPS and log score usually rank games alike. They part ways over how much a big surprise costs.
  • Choosing a distribution family is a question about tails, so expect the log score to have the stronger opinion.

Lesson 4 · Score

Scoring the intervals themselves: coverage and the interval score

Your dashboard and paper report 80% intervals and their coverage. Coverage checks the intervals but can't rank them. The interval score can.

Predict firstShrink every one of your 80% intervals by 30%. Coverage will drop. Will the average interval score, which rewards narrowness, go up or down?

4.1Coverage is a check, not a score

Coverage is the share of results inside the interval. Your margin intervals at 50%, 80% and 95% contain 49.8%, 79.2% and 94.5% of results, close to nominal. But coverage alone can be gamed. Report (−∞, ∞) for 80% of games and a single point for the rest, and you get perfect 80% coverage from a useless forecast. Coverage ignores width.

4.2The interval (Winkler) score

S = (u − l) + (2/α)(l − y)·1{y < l} + (2/α)(y − u)·1{y > u}α = 1 − level · the width, plus a penalty for each point the result falls outside

For an 80% interval, α = 0.2, so every point of miss costs 10 points.5,6 The score is proper for the interval's two quantiles,2 so on average the honest 10th and 90th percentiles win.

One family. The interval score is the sum of the two endpoints' pinball (quantile) losses, divided by α.5 Average the pinball loss over every quantile level and you get the CRPS (Lesson 2). Intervals, quantiles and the CRPS are the same idea at different resolutions.

On 2,500 FBS games, the multiplier that minimises the interval score is 1.00× at 50% and 1.05× at 80% and 95%. Your model's widths are about right, perhaps a hair narrow in the tails. Shrinking the 80% intervals to 0.7× raises the score from 57.2 to 63.0, and stretching them to 1.5× raises it to 64.3. Both directions cost.

Sharpness is the average width: 21.3, 40.9 and 63.7 points at 50%, 80% and 95%. Narrower is better, but only once coverage is right, which is the subject of Lesson 5.

Takeaways
  • Coverage checks calibration but ignores width, so it can't rank forecasts.
  • Interval score = width + (2/α) × distance outside. It is proper, and lower is better.
  • Your intervals are close to the score-minimising width at every level.

Lesson 5 · Diagnose

Calibration vs. sharpness: reading a PIT histogram

A score tells you how good a forecast is. A PIT histogram tells you what's wrong with it: too narrow, too wide, or shifted.

Predict firstA forecaster that ignores the teams and issues the same distribution, the spread of all historical margins, for every game: will its PIT histogram be flat?

5.1The probability integral transform

For each game, take u = F(y), the percentile at which the result fell within its own forecast. If the forecasts are ideal, the u values are uniform, so a histogram of them is flat.7 The Game had u = 0.12. The Orange Bowl, from Georgia's side, had u = 0.9975.

The histogram's shape is the diagnosis:7

  • U-shaped: too many results in the tails, so the forecasts are too narrow.
  • Hump-shaped: too many results near the centre, so the forecasts are too wide.
  • Sloped or triangular: the forecasts are biased. Too many high u values means the centre is set too low.

5.2Flat isn't enough

Look at Climatological and Climatology (real). A forecast that ignores the matchup entirely is perfectly calibrated: its PIT is flat and its 80% intervals hold 79–80%. It is also useless, with intervals 54 points wide against your model's 40, and a CRPS of 11.75 against 9.14. Gneiting, Balabdaoui & Raftery's rule is to maximise sharpness subject to calibration.7 Calibration is necessary. Among calibrated forecasts, the sharpest one wins. Proper scores such as the CRPS reward both at once, which is why the climatological forecaster scores worst in the simulation despite its flat histogram.

Integer margins. Real margins pile up on 3 and 7, so a step-function forecast like the real climatology needs a randomised PIT: draw u uniformly between F(y − 1) and F(y). Your model's forecast is continuous, so plain F(y) is fine. The paper's appendix makes the same point about the LOO-PIT.

Your model (all games): the histogram stays within a few percent of flat in every bin, and the coverage curve sits on the diagonal (70% intervals hold 69.3%, 90% hold 89.9%). There's a slight excess in the top two bins, meaning a few more home blowouts than forecast. That's worth watching, but it's small.

Takeaways
  • PIT = F(y). Uniform means calibrated. A U-shape means too narrow, a hump too wide, a slope biased.
  • Calibration is necessary but not sufficient. Climatology is calibrated and useless.
  • Maximise sharpness subject to calibration, and use a proper score to judge both together.

Lesson 6 · Compare

Is variant B really better? Paired differences and honest standard errors

Picking between model variants is half your mission. A mean score difference without the right standard error is just a guess.

Predict firstYour ablation set has 6,557 games. If you treat them as independent, will your standard errors be too small, about right, or too large?

6.1Compare on the same games, game by game

Score both models on identical games and take di = SB(i) − SA(i). Hard games are hard for both models, so pairing cancels most of the game-to-game noise and leaves the difference between the models.

6.2Your games aren't independent

One snapshot predicts all of a week's games. If it's off, it's off for every game that week, so errors arrive in week-sized clumps. The naive SE, sd(d)/√n, pretends you have 6,557 independent pieces of evidence. The clustered SE (your dm() at R/04_validation.R:247, in the spirit of Diebold–Mariano14) first adds up each season-week's deviations, then squares. That leaves 66 clumps, not 6,557 games.

Why clustering matters. Removing the carry-over hurts almost entirely in the first four weeks, when ratings still rest on last season. The difference arrives week by week, not game by game.

What to notice:

  • No carry-over is clearly worse: +0.65 points of CRPS per game. Its naive SE of 0.043 is 2.6× too small next to the clustered 0.111. That one isn't close either way, but with smaller effects, naive SEs produce false wins.
  • Normal, Student-t and margin-only variants beat the published model by about 0.045 points of CRPS per game (z between −4.4 and −5.1). The difference is real, and it is also 0.5% of a 9-point score. Decide in advance what size of difference would make you switch.
  • Toggle to log loss: it agrees in sign here. When CRPS and a log score disagree, Lesson 3 explains why.

6.3Skill scores: one number against a baseline

skill = 1 − Smodel / Sreference

Skill is the share of the reference's score that your model removes.5 On FBS–FBS games:

ReferenceReference CRPSModel CRPSCRPS skill

Takeaways
  • Compare paired, per-game score differences on the same games.
  • Cluster the SE by season-week. Naive SEs here are up to 2.6× too small.
  • Separate "real" (a large z) from "matters" (the size of the effect). Skill scores put the size in context.
